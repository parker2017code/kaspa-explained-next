No. 01

The pile-up problem

or, why Bitcoin waits ten minutes on purpose

A blockchain is a shared notebook. Every so often a computer somewhere wins the right to write the next page (a block) and shouts it to everyone else. Shouting takes time to cross the planet.

If two computers write a page at nearly the same moment, an ordinary blockchain can keep only one. The other page goes in the bin, with all the electricity spent making it. Bitcoin avoids the pile-up by writing one page about every ten minutes. Safe, and slow.

Kaspa's answer: stop binning. Each new block points back to every recent block its maker has heard of, so pages written at the same moment just sit side by side. The shape that makes is called a blockDAG, a family tree where a child can have many parents. Since May 5, 2025 the main network has aimed for ten blocks a second.2

Drag the speed up. Watch the chain on top start throwing pages away. The DAG underneath keeps every one.

No. 02

Who was first?

the group photo rule

Keeping every block makes a new headache. If two blocks each try to spend the same coin, which one wins? You need everyone to agree on an order.

Kaspa uses a rule called GHOSTDAG.1 Think of a group photo. Honest computers publish straight away, so their blocks all see each other and huddle together. They're coloured blue. A block that was mined in secret and pushed out later didn't see anyone, and nobody saw it. It doesn't fit in the photo, so it's coloured red and goes to the back of the queue. If it clashes with a blue block, it loses.

The k slider is how much "we were busy at the same time" the rule forgives. The real network uses k = 124, sized for news taking up to 5 seconds to spread.3 This toy uses a small k so you can see it work.

Go on, try to cheat. The longer you hide, the redder it gets.

No. 03

Five friends pay at once

coins, not a running tab

There are two ways to keep track of money. A running tab: one balance per person, updated line by line. Or a wallet of coins: each coin is its own object, and a payment says exactly which coins it uses up.

Kaspa, like Bitcoin, uses coins (the jargon is "UTXOs"). Because every payment names its coins, a computer can see at a glance that five payments touching five different coins can't interfere, and check them side by side. Two payments grabbing the same coin get caught instantly.

Press go. Then press it again with a double-spender in the queue.

No. 04

How hard is it to cheat?

priced in effort, not euros

To fake history you'd have to out-build everyone else, and "building" here means guessing. Miners race to find a number that makes a block's fingerprint come out right. There's no shortcut; you just guess, really fast.

Right now all Kaspa miners together make about … guesses every second. If every person on Earth made one guess per second, it would take them … to match one second of that.

A shop that waits a few seconds before handing over the goods makes a cheat very unlikely unless the cheater controls a big slice of all mining. And after 12 hours, nodes treat a block as settled and refuse to rewrite it, no matter who asks.4

Slide your share past 50%. That's the cliff every proof-of-work network lives next to.

No. 05

Try to rob the savings jar

money with house rules, live since June 30, 2026

Normally, whoever holds the key can spend the coin. Full stop. Lose your key to a thief and it's gone.

Since the Toccata upgrade switched on (June 30, 2026), Kaspa coins can carry covenants: rules about where the money may go next, checked by every computer on the network.5 The rules travel with the coin, like terms printed on a banknote. A language for writing them, SilverScript, reached version 1.0 in September.6

Here's a savings jar with three house rules. Spend from it. Then try to rob it.

Even with the stolen key, the thief can't take more than pocket money.

Tokens are the same trick

A token (say, points for a game or a coin someone launches) can be built as a coin that carries a rulebook: "this coin also holds 25 of token X, and here's how it may move". On October 21, 2026 the shared rulebook for that, KCC-20, became final.7 It's an agreement between builders about formats, not a change to Kaspa itself, and it needed no further network upgrade. Final doesn't mean safe: anyone can launch a token, and most are junk.

No. 06

The money supply plays a scale

chromatic, descending, very slowly

New coins are how miners get paid. Kaspa's schedule is written in its code as a table,8 and it is weirdly musical. The first step paid out 440 KAS a second. 440 is concert A, the note orchestras tune to. Every month the payout drops by one semitone, the gap between neighbouring piano keys. Twelve semitones make an octave, so the payout halves every year.

Right now it's … KAS a second, which as a pitch is …. That's below what most speakers can play. The next key down arrives ….

No. 09

Reasons to be skeptical

a fan site that only cheers is useless

  • Speed costs something

    Ten blocks a second means more data for every computer that checks the network. Push too far and only big operators can afford to check.

    Watch for: lots of independent people running nodes.

  • Who pays miners later?

    The new-coin payout halves every year. Eventually fees have to cover security, and today they're tiny.

    Watch for: real fee income growing over years.

  • Electricity is the bill

    Proof of work buys security with power and chips. Faster blocks don't make that bill smaller.

    Nothing settles this. It's the design.

  • Mining is concentrated

    Few companies make the special mining chips, and mining pools cluster.

    Watch for: production spread across many hands over time.

  • Roadmaps aren't products

    The next big consensus upgrade (DAGKnight), "verifiable programs", and native finance apps are separate projects on separate timelines. A demo of one proves nothing about the others.

    Watch for: main-network releases people use twice.

  • Cool tech, real users?

    Cheap, fast payments are what apps need to exist. They aren't proof anyone wants those apps.

    Watch for: people coming back because it's better than an ordinary website.
